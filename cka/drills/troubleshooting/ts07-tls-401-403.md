# TS7 — The API server is up and refusing you: TLS, 401 and 403 are three faults

**Reflex** · **Core** · **10 min** · [`pair`](../../../strands/lab-topologies.md#pair) · Troubleshooting / Troubleshoot cluster components

> **Three refusals, three entirely different causes, and people treat them as one.** A **TLS** failure means the connection never formed — nobody has been identified. A **401** means the server does not know who you are. A **403** means it knows exactly who you are and says no. Reading the refusal correctly sends you to the right place immediately; misreading it sends you to RBAC when the problem is a certificate.

**Break it** — *pass 1 only.* Work on a **copy** of the kubeconfig (`KUBECONFIG=/tmp/bad.yaml`), never the real one.

1. **TLS**: point `certificate-authority-data` at a different CA, or an unrelated `server:` address. The error names the certificate.
2. **401**: strip the client credentials, or corrupt the client certificate so it no longer verifies.
3. **403**: a valid identity with no permissions — reuse the ServiceAccount from **A6**, or a user whose RoleBinding you have removed.

**Work it**

- **Read the error text precisely**, because each is distinctive and each names its own cause:
  - `x509: certificate signed by unknown authority` / `certificate has expired` → TLS. The connection died; no identity was ever established.
  - `Unauthorized` → 401. Authentication. Who are you?
  - `User "x" cannot list resource "pods" in API group "" in the namespace "y"` → 403. Authorization, and the message **contains the exact RBAC rule you are missing** — verb, resource, API group, namespace. That sentence is a specification; read it as one.
- **`kubectl auth whoami`** answers the 401 question directly, and **`kubectl auth can-i`** answers the 403 one, including `--as` to impersonate and `--list` for everything an identity may do. These three commands resolve most of this topic.
- **Then the certificate half**, which is the part the CKA actually asks for. `kubeadm certs check-expiration` lists every control-plane certificate with its expiry — including the client certificate inside `admin.conf`, which expires after **one year** and is the classic "my cluster broke and nobody touched it" fault. Read your own expiry dates now; it costs one command.
- **Renew** with `kubeadm certs renew <name>` or `all`, then **restart the affected static pods** — renewal writes new files and running processes hold the old ones until restarted. Forgetting the restart makes the renewal look like it did nothing.
- Inspect a certificate by hand once: `openssl x509 -in /etc/kubernetes/pki/apiserver.crt -noout -text | grep -A2 Validity`, and look at the **Subject** of a client certificate — `CN` is the username and `O` is the group. That is how certificate auth maps to RBAC, and it makes the 401/403 boundary concrete.

**Observe**

```sh
kubectl auth whoami
kubectl auth can-i list pods --as=system:serviceaccount:dev:app -n dev
sudo kubeadm certs check-expiration
sudo openssl x509 -in /etc/kubernetes/pki/apiserver.crt -noout -subject -issuer -dates
KUBECONFIG=/tmp/bad.yaml kubectl get pods       # read the exact refusal
```

**Done when** — you classify a refusal into TLS, 401 or 403 from its text alone, and you can read a certificate's remaining life in one command.

**Passes**

| Pass | What changes | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Clean. Produce all three, classify all three, check expiration. | 10 min |
| **2** | A kubeconfig with two faults at once. Fix in the right order. | 8 min |
| **3** | Cold, no notes. Classify one refusal and name the fix. | 5 min |

**Teardown** — delete the scratch kubeconfig, confirm the real one still works, and **leave `/etc/kubernetes/pki` exactly as you found it** unless you deliberately renewed.
